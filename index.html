WEEK 1 · FIRST SOUNDS

Start with one beautiful note

You do not need to know how to read music yet. We'll teach you what each symbol means, where your fingers go, how your bow moves, and what to listen for.

Today's goal: Play four steady D-string quarter notes at 60 beats per minute.

Your guided beginner lessons

1. Meet your violin
Learn

The violin has four strings: G, D, A, E, from lowest to highest pitch. G is closest to your face in normal playing position; E is the thinnest string.

Do

Rest the violin on your left collarbone with the chin rest beneath your jaw. Support it gently with your head, without squeezing. Keep shoulders relaxed. Your left hand should be free to move.

Check

Can you identify each string from G to E and hold the violin comfortably for 20 seconds?

2. Hold your bow
Learn

Your right hand controls the bow. The thumb is bent and contacts the stick near the frog. The middle and ring fingers drape over the stick, index finger guides weight, and curved pinky balances on top.

Do

Without touching the strings, practice five slow windshield-wiper motions with a relaxed hand. Keep the thumb and pinky curved, not locked.

Check

Can you move the bow with flexible fingers and no tension?

3. Your first open-string sound
Learn

Open string means you don't press any fingers down. The D string is the second string from the low G side.

Do

Set the bow midway between the bridge and fingerboard, perpendicular to the D string. Draw it slowly toward the tip (down-bow), then return toward the frog (up-bow). Use steady speed and light natural arm weight.

Hear

Tap the button for the reference pitch D4.

Check

Can you sustain a clean D-string sound for four seconds without scratching or touching another string?

4. What is a quarter note?
Learn

A quarter note (♩) lasts one beat. At 60 BPM, one beat lasts exactly one second. Four quarter notes means play four separate one-beat sounds.

See
♩ ♩ ♩ ♩

Count out loud: ONE · TWO · THREE · FOUR. Each note gets one count. The symbol has a filled oval notehead and a stem.

Hear

Listen to four evenly spaced beats, then repeat them on your open D string using alternating down-bow and up-bow strokes.

Practice

Place your bow on D. Count “1, 2, 3, 4.” Play one bow stroke on each count: down, up, down, up. Keep each note equally long. Repeat five times.

Check

How long does a quarter note last at 60 BPM?

5. Your first left-hand finger
Learn

On violin, fingers are numbered 1=index, 2=middle, 3=ring, 4=pinky. Open string is finger 0. Unlike guitar, the violin has no frets, so your ear guides placement.

Do

On the D string, play open D4, then gently place your index finger near the nut end to produce E4 (a whole step above D). Keep the finger curved and use a tuner or reference pitch to fine-tune the location.

6. Read your first staff note
Learn

Sheet music uses five lines. In treble clef, the lines from bottom to top are E4, G4, B4, D5, F5. Spaces are F4, A4, C5, E5. Your open D4 sits just below the bottom line.

Do

Say the line names aloud, then play your open D4 and compare it with the reference.

7. Bow direction and rhythm
Learn

Down-bow means moving from frog toward tip. Up-bow means tip toward frog. For four quarter notes, alternate the direction once per beat.

Do

At 60 BPM play down-up-down-up, four beats on D, rest for four beats, and repeat four times. Focus on smooth starts and stops.

8. Your first mini performance
Learn

Combine open D, E with finger 1, and four quarter notes. Your first exercise is D D E E, each note one beat.

Do

Listen to D and E separately. Then play D D E E at 60 BPM, one bow per note. Record yourself and check for steady timing and consistent pitch.

The 12-month journey

52 weeks across 12 foundations. The first eight lessons above are interactive teaching examples; later weeks are a curriculum preview, not yet complete guided instruction.

  1. Instrument setup and first sounds
  2. Rhythm and note reading
  3. First-position finger placement
  4. Scales and intonation
  5. Bow control and articulation
  6. Keys and musical phrasing
  7. String crossings and dynamics
  8. Expressive playing and vibrato preparation
  9. Intermediate rhythms and shifting preparation
  10. Position changes and technique
  11. Repertoire and musical interpretation
  12. Polish, recording, and performance
